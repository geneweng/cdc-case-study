Two papers, one route

Cycle Double Cover Domain Map

The 2026 proof of the cycle double cover conjecture never attacks cycles directly. It carries the question out of graphs, through flows and edge labels, into linear algebra over 𝔽2, and settles it there with a parity count. This page maps every domain the two papers pass through and the mapping that connects each pair. An appendix at the end is a short tutorial on the linear algebra over 𝔽2 that the blue lane uses.

Where the two writeups take different roads

The route is the same in both papers. Four legs are travelled with different vehicles, and each difference is a choice of domain: Oum swaps a cited theorem for a self-contained construction, and an abstract object for a concrete one.

Neighbouring domains the papers point to

Oum's closing section and the OpenAI introduction mention several other translations of the same problem. None is needed for the proof, but each is another domain the cycle double cover question can be moved into, sometimes gaining strength and sometimes losing it.

  • Surfaces → cycles. A strong embedding has every face boundary a cycle, and the face boundaries of a 2-cell embedding cover every edge twice. The strong embedding conjecture would imply the theorem; the theorem now settles it for cubic graphs. Planar graphs were the first known case, by facial cycles of blocks. Oum Conj. 2; OpenAI §1.
  • Colourings ⇔ covers. For loopless cubic graphs, 3-edge-colourable, having a 3-cycle double cover, and having a 4-cycle double cover are equivalent. The Petersen graph shows 4 layers are not always enough; the proof gives 8; the open conjecture asks for 5. Oum Thm 19, Conj. 20; OpenAI §1 (Szekeres).
  • Covers → flows. An orientable k-cycle double cover yields a nowhere-zero ℤk-flow, so the orientable 5-cover conjecture would imply Tutte's 5-flow conjecture. Oum Lemma 22, Conj. 21.
  • Flows → covers. The seven nonzero linear combinations of the three coordinate subgraphs of an 𝔽23-flow form a 7-cycle 4-cover; Seymour's 6-flow gives a 10-cycle 6-cover. Oum Thm 23 (Bermond, Jackson, Jaeger), Thm 24 (Fan).
  • Matchings ⇔ covers. Complementing each of six perfect matchings that cover every edge twice gives a 6-cycle 4-cover, so the Berge–Fulkerson conjecture is a cover statement in disguise. Oum Conj. 25, 26.
  • Graphs → binary matroids. The conjecture is equivalent to the same statement for binary matroids without coloops and without an F7* minor, so the theorem covers all regular matroids. The bounded-size versions do not transfer: the cographic matroid of Kn needs n cycles. Oum Thm 27 (Jamshy, Tarsi), §9.5.
  • Integer flows ⇔ group flows. Tutte's group-flow theorem lets OpenAI trade a nowhere-zero 8-flow for an 𝔽23-flow, the domain where characteristic 2 makes orientation irrelevant. OpenAI §2 (Tutte [9]).

What the pattern teaches

Every hop trades an existence question for a more rigid object. Bridgelessness becomes cubic vertices, cubic vertices become three trees, trees become a flow, a flow becomes coset conditions, cosets become a linear system, and the system becomes a duality check. None of these hops is deep on its own.

The payoff is the last step. Once the question is "does this vector lie in a column space?", it can be attacked by showing every certificate against it vanishes, and the certificate sum turns out to count each edge at both of its ends. The cover is a double cover, the field has characteristic 2, and a graph edge has exactly two ends. The proof works because those three twos are the same two.

Appendix · Linear algebra over 𝔽₂

Everything in the blue lane is linear algebra over the field with two elements. This appendix teaches exactly that much: what changes when the only scalars are 0 and 1, and where the proof leans on each change. It assumes a first course in linear algebra over the reals. Chips such as step 7 point to the rows of the mapping table above.

A.1Two scalars

𝔽2 is the set {0, 1} with addition and multiplication taken mod 2. Addition is exclusive-or, multiplication is "and". It is a field because the one nonzero element, 1, is its own inverse. Every theorem of linear algebra that is proved for an arbitrary field, which is most of them, holds here without change.

Three consequences of the top-left table do most of the work in the proof.

  • Minus is plus. Since 1 + 1 = 0, the additive inverse of 1 is 1, so −x = x for every scalar and every vector. Oum can write tu + tv − de and tu + tv + de interchangeably, and orientation of edges never matters. step 4
  • Twice anything is zero. x + x = 0 for every vector x. This single line is the whole of the double-counting finish. step 7
  • Sums are parities. Adding n copies of 1 gives n mod 2. Any count taken in 𝔽2 is a parity, which is why the certificate sum collapses to "how many are nonzero, mod 2". step 6

A.2Vectors are bit strings, and bit strings are sets

A vector in 𝔽2n is a string of n bits, added position by position. There are 2n of them. Read the bits as membership in a set: a subset S of an n-element ground set becomes its indicator vector, and the sum of two indicator vectors is the indicator of the symmetric difference S △ T, the elements in exactly one of the two sets. Linear algebra over 𝔽2 is therefore set algebra under symmetric difference, and a subspace is a family of sets closed under it.

The proof uses this with the ground set E(G). Edge sets in which every vertex has even degree are closed under symmetric difference, since degrees add mod 2, so they form a subspace, the cycle space. Oum builds each layer Fi as a symmetric difference of fundamental cycles, and it is automatically even. step 3

The proof's small space is Γ = 𝔽23: eight vectors, seven of them nonzero. A function φ : E(G) → Γ is three even edge sets read at once. Bit i of φ(e) says whether e ∈ Fi, so "nowhere-zero" means every edge lies in at least one of the three layers, and "sums to zero at every vertex" says each layer has even degree there.

A.3Spans and cosets: why every label has two elements

Over the reals the span of a nonzero vector is a whole line. Over 𝔽2 it has two points:

⟨p⟩ = {0, p}, x + ⟨p⟩ = {x, x + p}.

A coset of a one-dimensional subspace is a pair, the vector and its shift by p. That is the entire reason the edge labels Pe = tv + φ(f) + ⟨φ(e)⟩ are two-element sets. step 8 Membership in a coset is easy to test: y ∈ x + ⟨p⟩ exactly when x + y ∈ {0, p}. So the edge condition tu + tv ∈ de + ⟨φ(e)⟩ says nothing more than: tu + tv + de is either 0 or φ(e). step 4

A two-dimensional subspace has four elements: 0 and three nonzero vectors x, y, x + y, which sum to zero. The converse holds too. Three distinct nonzero vectors with x + y + z = 0 satisfy z = x + y, so they are precisely the nonzero part of a two-dimensional subspace. At a cubic vertex the three flow values are such a triple, and this is what makes φ(f) + ⟨φ(e)⟩ = φ(g) + ⟨φ(e)⟩ for the two other edges f, g. step 4

Bases, dimension, and rank behave exactly as over the reals, because their proofs use only field axioms. One bonus is counting: a k-dimensional subspace of 𝔽2n has exactly 2k elements.

The seven nonzero vectors of 𝔽23 and their seven two-dimensional subspaces. Each line, including the circle, passes through three points that sum to zero, and every pair of points lies on exactly one line. This is the Fano plane.

Solid blue line. A triple that could be the flow values at a cubic vertex: 100 + 010 = 110. The third value is forced by the other two.

Dashed line. The nonzero vectors orthogonal to 110, that is ⟨110⟩⊥ without 0. It passes through 110 itself, because 110 · 110 = 1 + 1 = 0. Over the reals no nonzero vector is orthogonal to itself. Here, any vector with an even number of ones is. See A.4.

A.4The dot product: parity of overlap

The standard dot product h · p = Σ hi pi is computed in 𝔽2, so it equals the number of positions where both vectors have a 1, mod 2. In set language it is the parity of |H ∩ P|. The orthogonal complement W⊥ of a subspace W is defined as usual, and two facts survive intact:

dim W⊥ = n − dim W, (W⊥)⊥ = W.

Both hold because the form is non-degenerate: the only vector orthogonal to everything is 0, since a vector with a 1 in position i has dot product 1 with the i-th standard basis vector. Oum uses both facts in Proposition 14. The first says ⟨pe⟩⊥ has dimension n − 1, so two basis vectors suffice when n = 3. The second turns "x ∈ ⟨pe⟩" into "r · x = 0 for every r in a basis of ⟨pe⟩⊥", which is a list of linear equations. step 5

What does not survive is geometry. A nonzero vector can be orthogonal to itself, so W and W⊥ may overlap, 𝔽2n is not in general W ⊕ W⊥, and there are no lengths, angles, or orthogonal projections. The proof never asks for any of these. It does quietly rely on the oddity once: the certificate condition he · φ(e) = 0 allows he = φ(e) whenever φ(e) has even weight.

A.5Solving a system: elimination without fractions

Gaussian elimination works over any field. Over 𝔽2 every nonzero pivot is 1, so no row is ever scaled, and the only row operation is "add one row to another", which is a bitwise exclusive-or. The smallest example that matters for this proof is a triangle.

Worked example · one bit per vertex on a triangle

Vertices u, v, w, edges e₁ = uv, e₂ = vw, e₃ = wu. Find bits xu, xv, xw with xu + xv = b₁, xv + xw = b₂, xw + xu = b₃. Rows are edges, columns are vertices.

u v w │ e₁ 1 1 0 │ b₁ e₂ 0 1 1 │ b₂ e₃ 1 0 1 │ b₃ r₃ ← r₃ + r₁ 0 1 1 │ b₁ + b₃ r₃ ← r₃ + r₂ 0 0 0 │ b₁ + b₂ + b₃ ← consistent only if this is 0

If b₁ + b₂ + b₃ = 0, back-substitute: xw is free, xv = b₂ + xw, xu = b₁ + b₂ + xw. Two solutions, differing by the all-ones vector, which is the kernel: adding the same bit to every vertex changes no edge sum.

If b₁ + b₂ + b₃ = 1, there is no solution, and the vector y = (1, 1, 1) that added the three rows together is the proof. Note why the rows cancelled: every column has exactly two 1s, because every edge has exactly two ends. This is the whole proof in miniature.

A.6Column space equals the orthogonal complement of the left nullspace

For an m × n matrix A, the column space C(A) = {Ax} is the set of right-hand sides b for which Ax = b is solvable. The left nullspace ker(Aᵀ) = {y : yᵀA = 0} is the set of ways to combine rows so that the left-hand sides cancel. Oum's Lemma 13 says

C(A) = ker(Aᵀ)⊥.

The proof is three lines. If Aᵀy = 0 then y · Ax = (Aᵀy) · x = 0, so C(A) ⊆ ker(Aᵀ)⊥. Both sides have dimension rank A: the left by definition, the right because dim ker(Aᵀ) = m − rank A and A.4 subtracts that from m. A subspace contained in another of the same dimension equals it.

Read as a recipe: Ax = b has a solution unless some y cancels every left-hand side while y · b = 1. Such a y is a certificate of infeasibility, and to prove solvability you show that every candidate certificate has y · b = 0. In the triangle example the only nonzero candidate is (1, 1, 1), and y · b = b₁ + b₂ + b₃. step 5

A.7Assembling the proof's system

Now the real thing, with n = 3. Each unknown tv is three bits, so there are 3|V| scalar unknowns. An edge condition tu + tv ∈ de + ⟨φ(e)⟩ is a coset condition, not an equation, but A.4 converts it: it holds exactly when r · (tu + tv) = r · de for both vectors r of a basis of ⟨φ(e)⟩⊥. Stack those two rows as a 2 × 3 block Re.

t_u t_v t_w … edge e = uv [ R_e R_e 0 … ] = R_e d_e 2 rows per edge, R_e = basis of ⟨φ(e)⟩⊥ edge f = vw [ 0 R_f R_f … ] = R_f d_f

In a cubic graph 2|E| = 3|V|, so A happens to be square. The proof never uses that, and the system is never uniquely solvable: adding one fixed vector to every tv preserves every equation, just as in the triangle.

A certificate is y ∈ ker(Aᵀ), two bits ye per edge. Set he = Reᵀ ye, a combination of the two rows for e, so he · φ(e) = 0. The block of Aᵀy at vertex v is Σe ∋ v he, so "Aᵀy = 0" says the he sum to zero at every vertex. And y · b = Σe he · de. Lemma 13 therefore says: the tv exist if and only if every family (he) with he · φ(e) = 0 and zero sum at each vertex has Σ he · de = 0. That is Proposition 14. step 5

The last move is local. Fix a vertex with edges e₁, e₂, e₃. Oum shows all six cross products hei · φ(ej), i ≠ j, equal one bit λ, and that λ is the parity of the number of nonzero hei. Two instances, with flow values φ(e₁) = 100, φ(e₂) = 010, φ(e₃) = 110 from the figure: step 6

λ = 1 · all three nonzero

h₁ = 011, h₂ = 101, h₃ = 110. Sum: 011 + 101 = 110, and 110 + 110 = 000. Each is orthogonal to its own flow value: 011·100 = 0, 101·010 = 0, 110·110 = 0.

Cross products: h₂ · φ(e₁) = 101·100 = 1, h₁ · φ(e₂) = 011·010 = 1, h₃ · φ(e₁) = 110·100 = 1. So λ = 1, and three nonzero vectors give parity 1.

λ = 0 · exactly two nonzero

h₁ = 001, h₂ = 001, h₃ = 000. Sum: 001 + 001 = 000. Orthogonality: 001·100 = 0, 001·010 = 0, and h₃ = 0.

Cross products: 001·100 = 0, 001·010 = 0, 000·100 = 0. So λ = 0, and two nonzero vectors give parity 0. All three he lie in ⟨100, 010⟩⊥ = {000, 001}, a line with one nonzero point.

The vertex's share of Σ he · de is Σe ∋ v he · cv,e, which is a sum of three cross products and hence λ. Adding over all vertices adds, for every edge with he ≠ 0, the bit 1 once at each end. By A.1, twice anything is zero, so the total is 0, every certificate vanishes, and the tv exist. step 7
