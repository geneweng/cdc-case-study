A short course in one theorem

The Cycle Double Cover Theorem

From bridgeless graphs to a parity count over 𝔽2

Every bridgeless graph has a list of cycles that covers each edge exactly twice. Conjectured in the 1970s and proved in 2026, the theorem is settled by carrying the question out of graph theory, through flows and edge labels, into linear algebra over the two-element field, and finishing it there with a count that comes out even. This course teaches the proof in that order: the technique first, the field second, the theorem third.

Who this is for

A reader who knows graph theory at the level of a first course (connectivity, edge cuts, spanning trees, Eulerian graphs, matchings and edge colourings) and linear algebra over the reals (subspaces, dimension, rank, the dot product, orthogonal complements, Gaussian elimination), but who has never worked over a finite field. Chapter 2 builds everything about 𝔽2 that the proof uses, from the addition table up.

Sources

The proof is due to OpenAI's model GPT 5.6 and was announced in a three-page note in July 2026. Sang-il Oum's exposition of August 2026 made it self-contained and pitched it at advanced undergraduates. This course follows Oum's route and, like Oum, takes exactly two theorems on faith: Fleischner's splitting lemma and the Tutte–Nash-Williams tree packing theorem. Everything else is proved. Where the two writeups differ, the difference is noted.

Part I · The technique
Chapter 1

Moving a problem between domains

A cycle double cover is a floppy object. It is a list of cycles of unspecified length and number, subject to one global counting condition. Nobody has a good way to build such a list directly in an arbitrary bridgeless graph, and the proof does not try. It never writes down a cycle. Instead it repeatedly changes the kind of object being sought, each time for something more rigid, until the object is a solution of a system of linear equations and its existence can be decided by a computation.

1.1The shape of a domain transfer

Each hop in the proof has the same two halves, and it is worth naming them before seeing nine examples.

  • A lifting lemma. A statement of the form “if the rigid object exists, then so does the floppy one.” The proof of such a lemma is usually a short construction that reads the floppy object off the rigid one: cycles off an Eulerian subgraph, Eulerian subgraphs off a labelling, a labelling off a vector.
  • A construction in the new domain. Once the target has been replaced, the question is asked again in the new domain, where more structure is available. Sometimes the answer is a cited theorem (tree packing), sometimes a definition (fundamental cycles), and once, at the very end, a genuine calculation.

The skill in such a proof is choosing the domains so that every lifting lemma is easy and the final calculation is trivial. In this proof the final calculation is a parity: a sum in 𝔽2 in which every term appears twice.

1.2The route

The figure below is the whole proof at a glance. Each box is a domain, a kind of object. Each arrow is a mapping that turns a question about the source into a question about the target. The top lane is where the theorem is stated, the middle lane is where the combinatorial structure lives, and the bottom lane, in blue, is where the only real work happens. Later chapters prove the arrows; the numbers on them are used as cross-references throughout, written as hop 5.

Read in order, the nine hops are these. The chapter that proves each is in brackets.

  1. Bridgeless graph → cubic 3-edge-connected graph. A minimum counterexample has no 2-edge-cut and no vertex of degree four or more. [Chapter 4]
  2. Cubic 3-edge-connected graph → three spanning trees with no common edge. Double every edge and pack trees. [Chapter 5]
  3. Three trees → nowhere-zero 𝔽23-flow. Each tree yields an even edge set containing every non-tree edge; the three indicator vectors are the flow. [Chapter 5]
  4. Flow → linear system over 𝔽2. Local two-element labels are automatic at each vertex; they must agree across each edge, and agreement is a linear condition. [Chapters 6, 7]
  5. Linear system → orthogonality check. A system is solvable exactly when the right-hand side is orthogonal to the left nullspace. [Chapters 2, 7]
  6. Orthogonality check → parity at one vertex. At a cubic vertex every would-be certificate of infeasibility contributes a single bit, the parity of the number of nonzero certificate vectors there. [Chapter 7]
  7. Parity → the system is solvable. Summing over vertices counts every edge at both ends, and twice anything is zero. [Chapter 7]
  8. Flow and solution → two-element edge labels. The labels are cosets of one-dimensional subspaces, which have exactly two elements. [Chapter 6]
  9. Two-element labels → cycle double cover. One Eulerian subgraph per element of 𝔽23, eight in all. [Chapter 6]

1.3Why the two-element field

Three features of the problem are all instances of the number two, and the proof works because a single algebraic setting makes them the same two.

  • The cover is double. Every edge is used exactly twice. A set that uses each element an even number of times is exactly a set whose indicator vector sums to zero over 𝔽2.
  • An edge has two ends. Anything counted once per edge-end is counted twice per edge. In 𝔽2, twice anything is zero.
  • The field has characteristic two. Here x = −x, so a flow needs no orientation, subtraction is addition, and the cycle space of a graph is a vector space whose vectors are edge sets and whose addition is symmetric difference.

The specific space 𝔽23 enters through the flow. A nowhere-zero flow with values in 𝔽23 is three even edge sets that together cover every edge, and it exists in every 3-edge-connected graph. Its eight values become the eight Eulerian layers of the final cover.

Part II · The tools
Chapter 2

Linear algebra over 𝔽2

Almost every theorem of a first linear algebra course is proved from the field axioms alone, and so holds over any field. What changes over 𝔽2 is not the theorems but the objects: subspaces are finite, a line has two points, and a nonzero vector can be orthogonal to itself. This chapter goes through the definitions once, notes what is different, and ends with the one proposition the proof needs, a criterion for the solvability of a system of equations indexed by the edges of a graph.

2.1The field with two elements

Definition 2.1

𝔽2 is the set {0, 1} with addition and multiplication taken modulo 2.

+01
001
110
×01
000
101

Addition is exclusive-or and multiplication is “and”. The field axioms hold; the only one worth checking is that every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse, and the only nonzero element is 1, which is its own inverse. Three consequences of the addition table are used constantly.

Lemma 2.2

In 𝔽2, and hence in any vector space over 𝔽2:

  1. −x = x for every x, so subtraction is addition.
  2. x + x = 0 for every x.
  3. A sum of n copies of 1 equals n mod 2. A sum of scalars is the parity of the number of 1s among them.
Proof. 1 + 1 = 0 gives (ii) for scalars, and (i) follows since x + x = 0 means x is its own negative. For vectors apply (i) and (ii) coordinatewise. (iii) is (ii) applied repeatedly. □

Item (ii) is the sentence on which the proof turns, at hop 7: anything counted once at each end of an edge is counted twice per edge, and twice anything is zero.

2.2Vectors are bit strings, and bit strings are sets

The vector space 𝔽2n consists of strings of n bits, added position by position. It has 2ⁿ elements. Its most useful reading is as the family of subsets of an n-element set.

Definition 2.3

For a finite set X and a subset S ⊆ X, the indicator vector χS ∈ 𝔽2X has a 1 in position x exactly when x ∈ S. The symmetric difference S △ T is the set of elements in exactly one of S and T.

Lemma 2.4

χS + χT = χS △ T. Consequently S ↦ χS is a bijection from subsets of X to 𝔽2X under which symmetric difference becomes vector addition, and a family of subsets closed under symmetric difference and containing the empty set is a subspace.

Proof. In position x the sum is 1 + 1 = 0 if x lies in both sets, 1 if it lies in exactly one, and 0 if in neither. □

Linear algebra over 𝔽2 is therefore set algebra under symmetric difference. The proof uses this with X = E(G), the edge set of a graph.

Definition 2.5

An edge set F ⊆ E(G) is even if every vertex is incident with an even number of edges of F, a loop counting twice. The set of even edge sets, viewed inside 𝔽2E(G), is the cycle space of G.

Lemma 2.6

The cycle space is a subspace of 𝔽2E(G). In particular the symmetric difference of any number of even edge sets is even.

Proof. The degree of v in S △ T is degS(v) + degT(v) − 2|S ∩ T ∩ δ(v)|, which is even when the first two terms are. The empty set is even. Apply Lemma 2.4. □

This is used at hop 3, where three even edge sets are built as symmetric differences of cycles. The proof's other space is small.

Definition 2.7

Γ = 𝔽23, the space of 3-bit strings. It has eight elements, seven of them nonzero.

A function φ : E(G) → Γ is the same thing as three edge sets F₁, F₂, F₃, where bit i of φ(e) records whether e ∈ Fi. “φ(e) ≠ 0 for all e” says the three sets cover E(G); “Σe ∈ δ(v) φ(e) = 0 for all v” says each Fi is even.

2.3Spans, cosets, and planes

For a subspace W′ of a vector space W and a vector x, the coset x + W′ is {x + w : w ∈ W′}. The span ⟨p⟩ of a single vector is {ap : a ∈ 𝔽2}. Over the reals a span of one nonzero vector is a line with infinitely many points. Here:

Lemma 2.8

Let p ≠ 0. Then ⟨p⟩ = {0, p}, the coset x + ⟨p⟩ = {x, x + p} has exactly two elements, and y ∈ x + ⟨p⟩ if and only if x + y ∈ {0, p}.

Proof. The only scalars are 0 and 1. The two elements x and x + p are distinct because p ≠ 0. Finally y = x + ap for some a if and only if x + y = ap, using −x = x. □

This is why the edge labels of hop 8 are two-element sets, and why the agreement condition of hop 4 reads “tu + tv + de is either 0 or φ(e)”.

Lemma 2.9

A k-dimensional subspace of 𝔽2n has exactly 2k elements. In particular a two-dimensional subspace consists of 0 and three nonzero vectors x, y, x + y, and these three sum to 0. Conversely, if x, y, z are distinct nonzero vectors with x + y + z = 0, then {0, x, y, z} is a two-dimensional subspace.

Proof. With respect to a basis, the elements of the subspace are the 2k choices of coefficients in {0, 1}, all distinct by linear independence. For the converse, z = x + y, and x, y are independent since ax + by = 0 with (a, b) ≠ (0, 0) would force x = 0, y = 0, or x = y. So {0, x, y, x + y} is the span of x and y. □

At a cubic vertex the three flow values are nonzero and sum to zero, so they are distinct and form the nonzero part of a plane in Γ (Lemma 5.7). The picture of Γ below is worth keeping in mind.

The seven nonzero vectors of Γ = 𝔽23 and its seven planes. Each line, including the circle, passes through three points that sum to zero; every pair of points lies on exactly one line. This configuration is the Fano plane.

Solid blue line. A triple that could be the flow values at a cubic vertex: 100 + 010 = 110. The third value is forced by the other two.

Dashed line. The nonzero vectors orthogonal to 110, that is ⟨110⟩⊥ without 0 (Section 2.4). It passes through 110 itself, because 110 · 110 = 1 + 1 = 0.

2.4The dot product: parity of overlap

Definition 2.10

For h, p ∈ 𝔽2n, the dot product is h · p = Σi hi pi, computed in 𝔽2. For a subspace W, its orthogonal complement is W⊥ = {h : h · w = 0 for all w ∈ W}.

By Lemma 2.2(iii), h · p is the parity of the number of positions where both vectors have a 1; in set language, the parity of |H ∩ P|. The two structural facts about complements that the proof needs both survive.

Lemma 2.11

For every subspace W of 𝔽2n, dim W⊥ = n − dim W and (W⊥)⊥ = W.

Proof. Let w₁, …, wk be a basis of W and let B be the k × n matrix with these rows. Then W⊥ = ker B, and rank B = k since its rows are independent. Rank–nullity, valid over any field, gives dim ker B = n − k. For the second claim, every w ∈ W is orthogonal to everything in W⊥, so W ⊆ (W⊥)⊥; both have dimension k by the first claim applied twice, so they are equal. □

Both are used in Proposition 2.15 below. The first says ⟨p⟩⊥ has dimension n − 1, so in Γ two vectors span it. The second turns “x ∈ ⟨p⟩” into “r · x = 0 for every r in a basis of ⟨p⟩⊥”, a list of linear equations.

Warning 2.12 what does not survive

Over the reals v · v = 0 forces v = 0. Over 𝔽2, v · v is the parity of the number of 1s in v, so every vector of even weight is orthogonal to itself: 110 · 110 = 0. Consequently W and W⊥ can overlap, 𝔽2n need not be W ⊕ W⊥, and there are no lengths, angles, or orthogonal projections. In 𝔽24 the subspace W = ⟨1100, 0011⟩ satisfies W = W⊥. The proof never needs any of the lost geometry, but it does use the oddity once without comment: the certificate condition he · φ(e) = 0 allows he = φ(e) when φ(e) has even weight.

2.5Solving a system: elimination without fractions

Gaussian elimination works over any field. Over 𝔽2 every nonzero pivot is 1, so no row is ever scaled, and the only row operation is adding one row to another, a bitwise exclusive-or. The smallest example that matters for this course is a triangle, because it already contains the mechanism of the final proof.

Example 2.13 · one bit per vertex on a triangle

Vertices u, v, w; edges e₁ = uv, e₂ = vw, e₃ = wu. Given bits b₁, b₂, b₃, find bits xu, xv, xw with xu + xv = b₁, xv + xw = b₂, xw + xu = b₃. Rows are edges, columns are vertices.

u v w │ e₁ 1 1 0 │ b₁ e₂ 0 1 1 │ b₂ e₃ 1 0 1 │ b₃ r₃ ← r₃ + r₁ 0 1 1 │ b₁ + b₃ r₃ ← r₃ + r₂ 0 0 0 │ b₁ + b₂ + b₃ ← consistent only if this is 0

If b₁ + b₂ + b₃ = 0, back-substitute: xw is free, xv = b₂ + xw, xu = b₁ + b₂ + xw. There are two solutions, differing by the all-ones vector, which spans the kernel: adding the same bit to every vertex changes no edge sum.

If b₁ + b₂ + b₃ = 1 there is no solution, and the vector y = (1, 1, 1) that added the three rows is the proof. The rows cancelled because every column has exactly two 1s: every edge has exactly two ends.

2.6Column space and left nullspace

For an m × n matrix A over a field 𝔽, write C(A) = {Ax : x ∈ 𝔽ⁿ} for the column space and ker(AT) = {y ∈ 𝔽m : ATy = 0} for the left nullspace. The first is the set of right-hand sides for which Ax = b is solvable; the second is the set of ways of combining rows so that the left-hand sides cancel.

Lemma 2.14 column space and left nullspaceOum, Lemma 13

Let 𝔽 be any field and A an m × n matrix over 𝔽. With respect to the standard dot product on 𝔽m,

C(A) = ker(AT)⊥.

Proof. If ATy = 0 then yTA = 0, so y is orthogonal to every column of A and hence to C(A). Thus C(A) ⊆ ker(AT)⊥. Both sides have dimension rank A: the left by definition, the right because dim ker(AT) = m − rank AT = m − rank A, and Lemma 2.11 (which holds verbatim over any field) subtracts this from m. A subspace contained in another of the same dimension equals it. □

In words: Ax = b has a solution unless some y cancels every left-hand side while y · b ≠ 0. Such a y is a certificate of infeasibility. To prove that a system is solvable, it suffices to show that every candidate certificate has y · b = 0. In Example 2.13 the only nonzero candidate is (1, 1, 1). This is hop 5.

2.7A consistency criterion for systems on a graph

The proof's system has one unknown vector per vertex and one coset condition per edge. The following proposition, stated over any field and any dimension, is the exact form in which Lemma 2.14 will be applied. It is pure linear algebra; the graph only supplies the index sets. Here δ(v) denotes the set of edges incident with v.

Proposition 2.15 elementary consistency criterionOum, Prop. 14

Let 𝔽 be a field, n ≥ 2, and W = 𝔽ⁿ with the standard dot product. Let G be a loopless graph. For every edge e let pe ∈ W be nonzero and let de ∈ W. The following are equivalent.

  1. There are vectors tv ∈ W, one per vertex, such that tu + tv ∈ de + ⟨pe⟩ for every edge e = uv.
  2. For every family (he)e ∈ E(G) of vectors in W satisfying he · pe = 0 for every edge e and Σe ∈ δ(v) he = 0 for every vertex v, one has Σe ∈ E(G) he · de = 0.

Proof. (i) ⇒ (ii). Suppose the tv exist and let (he) satisfy the two hypotheses of (ii). For each edge e = uv choose λe ∈ 𝔽 with tu + tv = de + λepe. Since he · pe = 0,

Σe he · de = Σe = uv he · (tu + tv) = Σv (Σe ∈ δ(v) he) · tv = 0,

where the middle equality regroups the sum by vertex: each edge e = uv contributes he · tu to the term for u and he · tv to the term for v.

(ii) ⇒ (i). For each edge choose a basis re,1, …, re,n−1 of ⟨pe⟩⊥, which has dimension n − 1 by Lemma 2.11, and let Re be the (n − 1) × n matrix with these rows. Since (⟨pe⟩⊥)⊥ = ⟨pe⟩, a vector x lies in ⟨pe⟩ if and only if Rex = 0. Hence condition (i) is equivalent to

Re(tu + tv) = Rede for all e = uv.

Form the block matrix A with block rows indexed by edges and block columns indexed by vertices, whose block in position (e, v) is Re if v is an end of e and 0 otherwise. With t = (tv)v and b = (Rede)e, condition (i) is precisely the solvability of At = b.

By Lemma 2.14 it remains to show b ⊥ ker(AT). Take y = (ye)e ∈ ker(AT) with ye ∈ 𝔽n−1, and put he = ReTye. Then he is a combination of the rows of Re, so he · pe = 0. The block of ATy indexed by a vertex v is Σe ∈ δ(v) ReTye = Σe ∈ δ(v) he, which is therefore 0. So (he) satisfies the hypotheses of (ii), and

yTb = Σe yeTRede = Σe he · de = 0.

Thus b ∈ ker(AT)⊥ = C(A), the system At = b has a solution, and (i) holds. □

Remark 2.16

The families (he) in (ii) are the certificates of infeasibility of Section 2.6, translated back from the rows of A to one vector per edge. Their two defining conditions are local: an edge condition, he ⊥ pe, and a vertex condition, zero sum at v. That locality is what makes the parity count of Chapter 7 possible.

Part III · The theorem
Chapter 3

Cycles, covers, and the statement

Graphs in this course are finite and may have loops and parallel edges. For a vertex v, δ(v) is the set of edges incident with v, and the degree d(v) counts a loop twice. A subgraph with vertex set V(G) is identified with its edge set, so “Eulerian subgraph” and “even edge set” (Definition 2.5) mean the same thing.

Definition 3.1

A graph is Eulerian if every vertex has even degree. A cycle is a connected 2-regular subgraph; a loop and a pair of parallel edges are cycles. A cycle double cover (CDC) of G is a list of cycles such that every edge of G lies in exactly two of them. A k-cycle double cover (k-CDC) is a list of at most k Eulerian subgraphs such that every edge lies in exactly two of them.

The two definitions are compatible because Eulerian subgraphs are unions of cycles.

Lemma 3.2

Every nonempty even edge set is a union of pairwise edge-disjoint cycles.

Proof. Induction on |F|. If F contains a loop or two parallel edges, that is a cycle C. Otherwise every vertex meeting F has degree at least 2 in F; take a longest path in (V, F), whose last vertex has a second F-neighbour, necessarily on the path, closing a cycle C. In either case F △ C = F ∖ C is even by Lemma 2.6 and smaller, so it is a disjoint union of cycles by induction. □
Corollary 3.3

A graph has a cycle double cover if and only if it has a list of Eulerian subgraphs covering every edge exactly twice. In particular a k-CDC yields a CDC.

Proof. Cycles are Eulerian, and Lemma 3.2 splits each Eulerian subgraph into cycles without changing how often any edge is covered. □
Definition 3.4

A bridge is an edge whose deletion increases the number of components. A graph is bridgeless if it has none.

Lemma 3.5

A bridge lies in no cycle. Hence a graph with a cycle double cover is bridgeless.

Proof. If e = uv lay on a cycle, the rest of the cycle would be a u–v path avoiding e, so deleting e would not separate u from v or increase the number of components. □

The theorem says that this necessary condition is sufficient.

Theorem 3.6 cycle double cover theoremOpenAI 2026; Oum, Thm 1

Every bridgeless graph has a cycle double cover.

The statement was conjectured independently by Szekeres (1973) for cubic graphs and by Seymour (1979) for all bridgeless graphs; an equivalent form is due to Itai and Rodeh (1978), and Tutte is also credited. It is trivially true for Eulerian graphs (take the graph twice) and was known early for planar graphs, by taking the facial cycles of each block, and for 3-edge-colourable cubic graphs, by the following observation of Szekeres.

Proposition 3.7 Szekeres

A 3-edge-colourable cubic graph has a 3-cycle double cover.

Proof. Let M₁, M₂, M₃ be the colour classes; each is a perfect matching and they partition E(G). Each of M₁ ∪ M₂, M₂ ∪ M₃, M₁ ∪ M₃ is 2-regular, hence Eulerian, and an edge of Mi lies in exactly the two unions that contain Mi. □

The Petersen graph is cubic, bridgeless, and not 3-edge-colourable, so any general proof must handle graphs for which Proposition 3.7 fails. The proof of Theorem 3.6 does so by producing what the original note calls a relaxed 3-edge-colouring (Remark 6.2). Chapter 8 shows, conversely, that a 3-CDC or a 4-CDC of a cubic graph forces 3-edge-colourability, so the eight layers the proof produces cannot in general be reduced to four.

Exercises

  2. Show that a graph with a cycle double cover in which every cycle has length at most 2 is a disjoint union of loops and parallel pairs, and find its cover.
  3. Let G be planar and 2-connected. Show that the boundaries of its faces form a cycle double cover. Where does 2-connectivity enter?

Chapter 4

Reducing to cubic 3-edge-connected graphs

The first domain transfer, hop 1, replaces an arbitrary bridgeless graph by one in which every vertex has exactly three edges and every edge cut has at least three edges. Both properties are obtained by looking at a smallest counterexample and showing it cannot have a small cut or a large degree. One cited lemma does the degree part.

Definition 4.1

For an integer k, a graph G with at least two vertices is k-edge-connected if for every X ⊆ V(G) with ∅ ≠ X ≠ V(G) there are at least k edges with one end in X and the other outside. A graph is cubic if every vertex has degree 3.

A connected graph is bridgeless if and only if it is 2-edge-connected.

Lemma 4.2 Fleischner's splitting lemmaFleischner 1990, III.26; Oum, Lemma 3

Let G be 2-edge-connected, let v be a vertex of degree at least four, and let e₁, e₂, e₃ be distinct edges at v with other ends v₁, v₂, v₃. If G − {e₁, e₂, e₃} is connected, then at least one of

(G − {e₁, e₂}) + v₁v₂ and (G − {e₂, e₃}) + v₂v₃

is 2-edge-connected.

The operation detaches two edges from v and joins their far ends by a new edge, so it reduces the degree of v by two and the number of edges by one. We take the lemma on faith; its proof is a case analysis on edge cuts.

Remark 4.3 choosing the three edges

If G is 3-edge-connected and d(v) ≥ 4, three edges as in Lemma 4.2 always exist. If G − v is connected, a spanning tree of G − v plus one edge at v is a spanning tree of G that omits at least three edges at v. If G − v is disconnected, each component sends at least three edges to v (since G is 3-edge-connected); a spanning tree of each component plus one edge from it to v again gives a spanning tree omitting at least three edges at v. Removing three omitted edges leaves the tree, so the graph stays connected.

Proposition 4.4 reductionZhang; Oum, Prop. 4

Theorem 3.6 holds if and only if it holds for cubic 3-edge-connected graphs.

Proof. Suppose the theorem fails and let G be a counterexample with |E(G)| minimum. Then G is loopless, since a loop could be deleted, the smaller graph covered, and the loop added twice as a cycle. And G is connected, since otherwise each component is smaller, bridgeless, and hence covered.

G is 3-edge-connected. Otherwise G has a 2-edge-cut {e₁, e₂}. Contract e₁ and call the merged vertex w. Contraction cannot create a bridge, so G/e₁ is a smaller bridgeless graph and has a cycle double cover 𝒞 by minimality. Consider the two shores of G − {e₁, e₂}. If e₂ is not a loop of G/e₁, then a cycle of 𝒞 containing e₂ passes through w from one shore to the other, and a cycle not containing e₂ cannot do so, since e₁ and e₂ were the only edges between the shores. Split w back into the two ends of e₁ and insert e₁ into the two cycles that contain e₂; all other cycles lift unchanged. If e₂ is a loop of G/e₁, replace its two occurrences by two copies of the 2-cycle {e₁, e₂}. Either way G has a cycle double cover, a contradiction.

G is cubic. Suppose d(v) ≥ 4 for some v. Choose e₁, e₂, e₃ by Remark 4.3 and apply Lemma 4.2 to get a 2-edge-connected graph H with fewer edges. By minimality H has a cycle double cover. Replace each occurrence of the new edge vivj by the path vi v vj through the two detached edges. The result is a list of closed trails covering each edge of G twice; decompose each into cycles by Lemma 3.2. This is a cycle double cover of G, a contradiction. Vertices of degree 1 do not occur in a bridgeless graph and degree 2 cannot occur in a 3-edge-connected graph with at least two vertices, so every vertex has degree exactly 3. □

From now on G is cubic and 3-edge-connected. Note that such a graph is loopless as soon as it has two vertices: a loop at v would leave a single edge as a cut. The payoff of cubicity is that every local identity in the rest of the proof has three terms, and the flow value on one edge at a vertex is the sum of the values on the other two.

Chapter 5

Three trees and a nowhere-zero flow

The middle lane begins here. Connectivity, a statement about all edge cuts, is exchanged for three concrete spanning trees (hop 2), and the trees are exchanged for a single function on edges with values in Γ = 𝔽23 that is nonzero everywhere and conserved at every vertex (hop 3). The second cited theorem does the first exchange.

Theorem 5.1 Tutte 1961, Nash-Williams 1961Oum, Thm 5

A graph G has t edge-disjoint spanning trees if and only if for every partition of V(G) into m nonempty parts there are at least t(m − 1) edges joining distinct parts.

The necessity is clear: each spanning tree needs m − 1 edges across any m-part partition. We take the sufficiency on faith; short proofs exist via the matroid union theorem or directly (Kaiser 2012).

Corollary 5.2 Kundu 1974Oum, Cor. 6

A 2k-edge-connected graph has k edge-disjoint spanning trees.

Proof. Let V₁, …, Vm be a partition into nonempty parts. Each Vi has at least 2k edges leaving it. Summing over i counts each crossing edge twice, so there are at least km ≥ k(m − 1) crossing edges. Apply Theorem 5.1. □
Lemma 5.3 three trees with no common edgeJaeger; Oum, Lemma 7

A 3-edge-connected graph G has spanning trees T₁, T₂, T₃ with E(T₁) ∩ E(T₂) ∩ E(T₃) = ∅.

Proof. Let G′ be obtained from G by adding a parallel copy of every edge. Every cut of G′ has twice as many edges as the corresponding cut of G, so G′ is 6-edge-connected, and Corollary 5.2 gives three edge-disjoint spanning trees of G′. Replacing each copy by its original edge yields spanning trees T₁, T₂, T₃ of G. An edge of G has only two copies in G′, so it cannot lie in all three trees. □

Each tree now produces an even edge set covering the tree's complement. This is where the cycle space of Section 2.2 is used.

Lemma 5.4 Oum, Lemma 8

Let T be a spanning tree of G. There is an even edge set F with E(G) ∖ E(T) ⊆ F.

Proof. For each non-tree edge e let Ce be the unique cycle in T + e, the fundamental cycle of e. Let F be the symmetric difference of all the Ce, that is, the set of edges lying in an odd number of them. It is even by Lemma 2.6. Each non-tree edge e lies in Ce and in no other fundamental cycle, so it lies in F. □
Definition 5.5

Let Γ be an abelian group in which x = −x for all x, such as 𝔽23. A nowhere-zero Γ-flow of G is a function φ : E(G) → Γ with φ(e) ≠ 0 for every edge and Σe ∈ δ(v) φ(e) = 0 for every vertex v.

Remark 5.6 orientation

For a general abelian group a flow is defined on an oriented graph, with inflow equal to outflow at each vertex. Reversing an edge negates its value. When x = −x the orientation is invisible, which is why Definition 5.5 needs none. This is the first place where characteristic 2 pays.

Lemma 5.7 nowhere-zero 𝔽23-flowJaeger, Kilpatrick; Oum, Lemma 10

Every 3-edge-connected graph has a nowhere-zero Γ-flow, Γ = 𝔽23. If the graph is cubic, then at every vertex the three flow values are nonzero, pairwise distinct, sum to zero, and form the nonzero part of a two-dimensional subspace of Γ.

Proof. Take T₁, T₂, T₃ from Lemma 5.3 and, for each i, an even set Fi ⊇ E(G) ∖ E(Ti) from Lemma 5.4. Define φ(e) to be the 3-bit string whose i-th bit is 1 exactly when e ∈ Fi. Each coordinate sums to zero at every vertex because each Fi is even. Every edge is omitted by some Ti and therefore lies in Fi, so φ(e) ≠ 0. At a cubic vertex with edges e₁, e₂, e₃, the sum is zero and each value is nonzero, so φ(e₁) = φ(e₂) would force φ(e₃) = 0; hence the three values are distinct, and Lemma 2.9 makes them the nonzero part of a plane. □
Remark 5.8 the other road to the flow

Jaeger and Kilpatrick proved independently in the 1970s that every 2-edge-connected graph has a nowhere-zero 𝔽23-flow, using Theorem 5.1 in essentially this way. By Tutte's theorem that a graph has a nowhere-zero flow in one abelian group of order k if and only if it has one in every abelian group of order k, if and only if it has a nowhere-zero integer k-flow, this is the “8-flow theorem”. The original note cites the 8-flow theorem and Tutte's result; Oum's construction above avoids integer flows entirely, and only needs the 3-edge-connected case that Chapter 4 provides. Seymour's 6-flow theorem is stronger but not needed.

Chapter 6

From two-element labels to a cover

This chapter proves the two lifting lemmas at the right-hand end of the route, hop 9 and hop 8. Both are short. The first turns a labelling of edges by pairs into a cover; the second turns a flow together with one vector per vertex into such a labelling, and isolates the single condition that the vertex vectors must satisfy. Chapter 7 then shows the condition can be met.

Lemma 6.1 two-element edge labelsOpenAI, Lemma 2.1; Oum, Lemma 11

Let G be a loopless graph and Γ a finite set. Suppose every edge e is assigned a two-element set Pe ⊆ Γ such that, for every vertex v and every s ∈ Γ, the number of edges e ∈ δ(v) with s ∈ Pe is even. Then G has a cycle double cover with at most |Γ| Eulerian subgraphs.

Proof. For s ∈ Γ let Hs = {e : s ∈ Pe}. The hypothesis says every vertex has even degree in Hs, so Hs is Eulerian. Each edge e lies in exactly two of the Hs, namely those with s ∈ Pe, since |Pe| = 2. Apply Corollary 3.3. □
Remark 6.2 a relaxed 3-edge-colouring

A proper 3-edge-colouring of a cubic graph is a labelling of this kind. Take distinct nonzero a, b ∈ Γ and assign {0, a}, {0, b}, {a, b} to the three colour classes; at each vertex each of 0, a, b appears exactly twice. Lemma 6.1 asks only for the local evenness that a colouring provides, without asking the same three sets to be used everywhere. That relaxation is what lets the proof pass through snarks.

In a cubic graph with a nowhere-zero Γ-flow, local labels satisfying the evenness condition at a single vertex come for free. Fix a vertex v with edges e, f, g and any t ∈ Γ, and consider the three sets

{t + φ(f), t + φ(g)}, {t + φ(e), t + φ(g)}, {t + φ(e), t + φ(f)},

assigned to e, f, g respectively. Each of the three values t + φ(e), t + φ(f), t + φ(g) appears in exactly two of the sets, and by Lemma 5.7 these three values are distinct, so no other value appears at all. The set assigned to e is the coset t + φ(f) + ⟨φ(e)⟩, because φ(g) = φ(f) + φ(e). The difficulty is that the other end of e builds its own set, from its own t and its own other edges, and the two sets must coincide. The next lemma says that this is the only difficulty.

Lemma 6.3 flow liftingOum, Lemma 12

Let G be a cubic loopless graph, Γ = 𝔽23, and φ a nowhere-zero Γ-flow. Suppose there are tv ∈ Γ for all vertices v such that, for every edge e = uv and all fu ∈ δ(u) ∖ {e} and fv ∈ δ(v) ∖ {e},

tu + tv ∈ (φ(fu) + φ(fv)) + ⟨φ(e)⟩.

Then G has an 8-cycle double cover.

Proof. First, the condition does not depend on the choices of fu and fv: if e, f, g are the edges at a vertex then φ(e) + φ(f) + φ(g) = 0, so φ(f) + ⟨φ(e)⟩ = φ(g) + ⟨φ(e)⟩ by Lemma 2.8.

For each edge e = uv define Pe = tu + φ(fu) + ⟨φ(e)⟩. By hypothesis tu + φ(fu) and tv + φ(fv) differ by an element of ⟨φ(e)⟩, so they lie in the same coset and Pe = tv + φ(fv) + ⟨φ(e)⟩ as well. Thus Pe is well defined from either end, and |Pe| = 2 by Lemma 2.8 since φ(e) ≠ 0.

At a vertex v with edges e, f, g, computing each coset from the end v gives exactly the three sets displayed above with t = tv. So every element of Γ appears in 0 or 2 of them, and Lemma 6.1 applies with |Γ| = 8. □

The hypothesis of Lemma 6.3 is one condition per edge, on unknowns indexed by vertices, and by Lemma 2.8 each condition is a coset membership in Γ. That is a linear system over 𝔽2. Everything that remains is to solve it.

Chapter 7

The linear system and the parity count

This is the blue lane, hops 4 to 7, and it is where the proof is decided. The system from Lemma 6.3 is written in the form of Proposition 2.15, the proposition converts solvability into a statement about certificates, and a computation at a single cubic vertex shows that every certificate contributes a parity that the two ends of each edge cancel.

7.1Setting up the system

Let G be cubic and 3-edge-connected, Γ = 𝔽23, and φ a nowhere-zero Γ-flow from Lemma 5.7. If v is an end of e, choose either edge f ∈ δ(v) ∖ {e} and set

cv,e = φ(f).

The two possible choices differ by φ(e), so the coset cv,e + ⟨φ(e)⟩ is independent of the choice. For each edge e = uv set de = cu,e + cv,e. By Lemma 6.3 it suffices to find tv ∈ Γ with

(★)tu + tv ∈ de + ⟨φ(e)⟩ for every edge e = uv.

This is condition (i) of Proposition 2.15 with 𝔽 = 𝔽2, n = 3, W = Γ and pe = φ(e), which is nonzero as required. So (★) is solvable if and only if condition (ii) holds: for every family (he) of vectors in Γ with

(†)he · φ(e) = 0 for all e and Σe ∈ δ(v) he = 0 for all v,

one has Σe he · de = 0. That is hop 5. Fix such a family for the rest of the chapter.

Remark 7.1 the matrix, for the record

Unwinding Proposition 2.15, the unknown vector t has 3|V| bits, and each edge contributes the two rows Re, a basis of ⟨φ(e)⟩⊥, in the two block columns of its ends. In a cubic graph 2|E| = 3|V|, so the matrix is square. The proof never uses that, and the solution is never unique: adding one fixed vector to every tv preserves (★), exactly as the all-ones vector did in Example 2.13.

7.2One vertex, one bit

Fix a vertex v and write δ(v) = {e₁, e₂, e₃}, with hi = hei and φi = φ(ei) for short. By Lemma 5.7 the φi are nonzero, distinct, and

φ₁ + φ₂ + φ₃ = 0, h₁ + h₂ + h₃ = 0, hi · φi = 0.

Lemma 7.2 the six cross products agreeOum, proof of Prop. 15; OpenAI, eq. (7)–(9)

Put λ = h₂ · φ₁. Then

λ = h₂ · φ₁ = h₂ · φ₃ = h₁ · φ₃ = h₁ · φ₂ = h₃ · φ₂ = h₃ · φ₁,

and λ is the parity of the number of nonzero vectors among h₁, h₂, h₃.

Proof. Dot each hi with φ₁ + φ₂ + φ₃ = 0 and use hi · φi = 0; dot h₁ + h₂ + h₃ = 0 with each φj and use the same fact:

h₁·φ₂ + h₁·φ₃ = 0, h₂·φ₁ + h₂·φ₃ = 0, h₃·φ₁ + h₃·φ₂ = 0,

h₂·φ₁ + h₃·φ₁ = 0, h₁·φ₂ + h₃·φ₂ = 0, h₁·φ₃ + h₂·φ₃ = 0.

In 𝔽2 each equation says its two terms are equal, and the six equalities chain the six cross products together: h₂·φ₁ = h₂·φ₃ = h₁·φ₃ = h₁·φ₂ = h₃·φ₂ = h₃·φ₁.

If λ = 1, each hi has a nonzero dot product with something, so all three are nonzero: the count is 3, parity 1. If λ = 0, each hi is orthogonal to φ₁, φ₂ and φ₃, which span a plane U by Lemma 5.7; so hi ∈ U⊥, which has dimension 1 by Lemma 2.11 and hence is {0, w} for a single nonzero w. The three hi are each 0 or w and sum to 0, so an even number equal w: parity 0. □

Now compute the vertex's share of the certificate sum. Choose cv,e₁ = φ₂, cv,e₂ = φ₁, cv,e₃ = φ₁. Any other choice changes cv,ei by φi, and hi · φi = 0, so the dot products below do not depend on the choice. By Lemma 7.2,

(‡)Σe ∈ δ(v) he · cv,e = h₁·φ₂ + h₂·φ₁ + h₃·φ₁ = λ + λ + λ = λ = Σe ∈ δ(v) 𝟙[he ≠ 0],

where 𝟙[he ≠ 0] is 1 if he ≠ 0 and 0 otherwise, and the last sum is taken in 𝔽2. This is hop 6: a computation in a three-dimensional space has become a count of nonzero edges at a vertex.

Example 7.3 · λ = 1, all three nonzero

Flow values φ₁ = 100, φ₂ = 010, φ₃ = 110, the solid line of the Fano figure. Take h₁ = 011, h₂ = 101, h₃ = 110. Sum: 011 + 101 = 110, and 110 + 110 = 000. Each is orthogonal to its own flow value: 011·100 = 0, 101·010 = 0, 110·110 = 0.

Cross products: h₂·φ₁ = 101·100 = 1, h₁·φ₂ = 011·010 = 1, h₃·φ₁ = 110·100 = 1. So λ = 1, and three nonzero vectors have parity 1.

Example 7.4 · λ = 0, exactly two nonzero

Same flow values. Take h₁ = 001, h₂ = 001, h₃ = 000. Sum: 001 + 001 = 000. Orthogonality: 001·100 = 0, 001·010 = 0, and h₃ = 0.

Cross products: 001·100 = 0, 001·010 = 0, 000·100 = 0. So λ = 0, and two nonzero vectors have parity 0. Here U⊥ = ⟨100, 010⟩⊥ = {000, 001}, and both nonzero hi equal its one nonzero element, as the proof of Lemma 7.2 predicts.

7.3Every edge has two ends

Theorem 7.5 OpenAI, Lemma 2.2; Oum, Prop. 15

Every cubic 3-edge-connected graph has an 8-cycle double cover.

Proof. With φ, cv,e, de as in Section 7.1, it suffices by Lemma 6.3 to solve (★), and by Proposition 2.15 it suffices to show Σe he · de = 0 for every family (he) satisfying (†). Since de = cu,e + cv,e for e = uv, the sum regroups by vertex, and (‡) applies at each:

Σe ∈ E(G) he · de = Σv Σe ∈ δ(v) he · cv,e = Σv Σe ∈ δ(v) 𝟙[he ≠ 0] = 2 · Σe 𝟙[he ≠ 0] = 0.

The third equality holds because every edge with he ≠ 0 is counted once at each of its two ends, and the last because 2 = 0 in 𝔽2 (Lemma 2.2). So (★) has a solution, Lemma 6.3 yields the labels Pe, and Lemma 6.1 yields the eight Eulerian layers Hs, s ∈ Γ. □

This is hop 7, and the moment where the three twos of Section 1.3 coincide. Assembling the chapters:

Theorem 7.6 = Theorem 3.6

Every bridgeless graph has a cycle double cover.

Proof. By Proposition 4.4 it is enough to treat cubic 3-edge-connected graphs, and Theorem 7.5 does. □
Theorem 7.7 Oum, Thm 18

Every bridgeless graph has an 8-cycle double cover.

Proof. Run the reduction of Proposition 4.4 with “8-cycle double cover” in place of “cycle double cover”. Each lifting step there (adding a loop twice, re-inserting e₁, replacing the new edge by a path) maps Eulerian subgraphs to Eulerian subgraphs without increasing their number; adding a loop twice can be done inside two existing layers, or as two new layers if the graph is a single loop. Theorem 7.5 provides the base case. □

7.4The same computation in the original note

Remark 7.8

The original note fixes at each vertex a local order a, b, c of the edges and sets gv,a = 0, gv,b = φ(a), gv,c = 0, so that only the edge b contributes at v. The agreement condition then reads tu + tv + εe φ(e) = de with an extra unknown bit εe per edge recording which of the two shifts occurs; Oum's coset absorbs that bit. Solvability is argued through the dual space Γ*: a vector lies in the image of a linear map if every functional vanishing on the image vanishes on it, which is Lemma 2.14 without the matrix. The vertex computation is then the same as Lemma 7.2, with the functionals ηe in place of the vectors he, and ends with the same sentence: “each edge with ηe ≠ 0 occurs twice in the last sum, once at each endpoint.”

Chapter 8

Neighbouring domains and open conjectures

The theorem sits among several other formulations of the same question, some equivalent, some stronger, some weaker. Each is another domain into which the cycle double cover problem can be moved. None was needed for the proof, but together they explain what the proof does and does not settle. The figure collects them; the sections that follow prove what is short and state what is not.

8.1Colourings and small covers

Theorem 8.1 JaegerOum, Thm 19

For a loopless cubic graph G the following are equivalent: (i) G is 3-edge-colourable; (ii) G has a 3-cycle double cover; (iii) G has a 4-cycle double cover.

Proof. (i) ⇒ (iii): Proposition 3.7 gives a 3-CDC, which is also a 4-CDC.

(iii) ⇒ (ii): If C₁, …, C₄ is a 4-CDC, then C₁ △ C₂, C₁ △ C₃, C₁ △ C₄ are Eulerian by Lemma 2.6, and an edge lies in exactly two of them: an edge in C₁ and Cj lies in the two differences not involving Cj, and an edge in Ci and Cj with i, j ≠ 1 lies in exactly those two.

(ii) ⇒ (i): Let C₁, C₂, C₃ be a 3-CDC. At a vertex the three degrees are even and sum to 6, and each is at most 3, so each is exactly 2: every Ci omits exactly one edge at each vertex. Every edge is omitted by exactly one Ci, since it lies in two of three. Colour each edge by the index of the layer omitting it; at each vertex the three edges are omitted by three different layers, so the colouring is proper. □

The Petersen graph is not 3-edge-colourable, so it has no 4-cycle double cover, and eight layers cannot always be reduced to four. The gap between four and eight is the subject of one of the main open problems.

Conjecture 8.2 Celmins 1985, Preissmann 1981Oum, Conj. 20

Every bridgeless graph has a 5-cycle double cover.

A different notion of smallness counts cycles rather than layers. Bondy conjectured in 1990 that every simple 2-edge-connected graph on n vertices has a cycle double cover with at most n − 1 cycles, which would be tight for Kn; the method of this course does not address it. His companion conjecture for cubic graphs is now a theorem: Lai, Yu and Zhang showed in 1994 that a simple 2-connected cubic graph on n vertices other than K₄ with some cycle double cover has one with at most n/2 cycles, and Theorem 3.6 supplies the hypothesis.

8.2Orientable covers and integer flows

Definition 8.3

An orientable cycle double cover is a list of Eulerian subgraphs, each given an orientation in which every vertex has equal in- and out-degree, such that every edge of G is used exactly once in each direction. An orientable k-CDC has at most k members.

Conjecture 8.4 Archdeacon 1984, Jaeger 1988Oum, Conj. 21

Every bridgeless graph has an orientable 5-cycle double cover.

This would imply Tutte's 5-flow conjecture, because orientable covers produce integer flows.

Lemma 8.5 Zhang 2012, Lemma 13.1.5; Oum, Lemma 22

If G has an orientable k-cycle double cover, then G has a nowhere-zero ℤk-flow.

Proof. Pad the list to exactly k members C₁, …, Ck with empty subgraphs. Fix a reference orientation of G and let χi(e) be +1, −1 or 0 according as Ci uses e forwards, backwards, or not at all. Each χi is an integer flow because Ci is Eulerian as an oriented graph. Hence φ = Σi i χi mod k is a ℤk-flow. For each edge e there are i ≠ j with Ci and Cj using e in opposite directions and no other member using it, so φ(e) = ±(i − j) ≠ 0 in ℤk. □

8.3Covering more than twice

Definition 8.6

An m-cycle k-cover is a list of m Eulerian subgraphs such that every edge lies in exactly k of them.

The 𝔽23-flow of Chapter 5 gives such a cover directly, by the dot-product counting of Section 2.4.

Theorem 8.7 Bermond, Jackson, Jaeger 1983Oum, Thm 23

Every bridgeless graph has a 7-cycle 4-cover.

Proof. Treat components separately and cover loops twice within two layers, so assume G is 2-edge-connected. By Jaeger's theorem (Remark 5.8) let φ be a nowhere-zero 𝔽23-flow and let C₁, C₂, C₃ be the supports of its three coordinates, each Eulerian. For each nonzero s ∈ 𝔽23 let Cs be the symmetric difference of the Ci with si = 1; it is Eulerian by Lemma 2.6, and an edge e lies in Cs exactly when s · φ(e) = 1. Since φ(e) ≠ 0, the vectors s with s · φ(e) = 0 form ⟨φ(e)⟩⊥, which has 4 elements, 3 of them nonzero, by Lemmas 2.9 and 2.11. So e lies in exactly 7 − 3 = 4 of the seven Cs. □

Fan showed in 1992, using Seymour's 6-flow theorem, that every bridgeless graph has a 10-cycle 6-cover. The conjecture in this family is the following.

Conjecture 8.8 Berge, Fulkerson 1971Oum, Conj. 25, 26

Every bridgeless cubic graph has six perfect matchings such that every edge lies in exactly two of them. Equivalently, taking complements, every bridgeless cubic graph has a 6-cycle 4-cover.

The equivalence holds because the complement of a perfect matching in a cubic graph is 2-regular, and an edge lies in exactly two of six matchings if and only if it lies in exactly four of the six complements.

8.4Matroids and surfaces

The proof is carried out with edge sets and 𝔽2-linear algebra, so it is natural to ask whether it lives in the larger world of binary matroids, where “cycle” means a disjoint union of circuits and the graph is forgotten. Jamshy and Tarsi showed in 1989 that the cycle double cover conjecture is equivalent to the following apparently stronger statement, which is now a theorem.

Theorem 8.9 Jamshy, Tarsi 1989, with Theorem 3.6Oum, Thm 27

Every binary matroid without coloops and without an F₇* minor has a cycle double cover.

Here F₇* is the dual of the Fano matroid, whose seven points are the seven nonzero vectors of Γ in the figure of Section 2.3. Every regular matroid is binary without an F₇* minor, so the theorem covers all regular matroids without coloops. The bounded versions do not transfer: Linial, Meshulam and Tarsi showed that the cographic matroid of Kn needs n cycles in any cycle double cover, so no analogue of Conjecture 8.2 with a fixed number of cycles can hold for regular matroids.

Finally, the original motivation. A strong embedding of a graph on a surface is a 2-cell embedding in which every face boundary is a cycle. The strong embedding conjecture, that every 2-connected graph has one, would imply Theorem 3.6 by taking face boundaries; it remains open in general, and Theorem 3.6 is known to settle it for cubic graphs.

  2. Show that the eight layers of Theorem 7.5 can be reduced to a 7-cycle double cover by removing the layer H₀ if it is empty, and that H₀ can be made empty by a translation as in Exercise 7.2 whenever some element of Γ lies in no Pe. Why does this not give a 7-CDC in general?

Epilogue

What the pattern teaches

Every hop of the proof trades an existence question for a more rigid object. Bridgelessness becomes cubic vertices, cubic vertices become three trees, trees become a flow, a flow becomes coset conditions, cosets become a linear system, and the system becomes a duality check. None of these hops is deep on its own, and each is a lemma of a few lines.

The payoff is the last step. Once the question is “does this vector lie in a column space?”, it can be attacked by showing every certificate against it vanishes, and the certificate sum turns out to count each edge at both of its ends. The cover is a double cover, the field has characteristic 2, and a graph edge has exactly two ends. The proof works because those three twos are the same two.

For a reader who came for the linear algebra: everything used from Chapter 2 was elementary, and everything that was different from the real case was a simplification. Lines have two points, elimination needs no fractions, and a sum over both ends of every edge is zero. The difficulty of the conjecture lay not in the algebra but in finding the sequence of domains that made the algebra this easy.
